DEENTR

Day 17 · October 4, 2009

Kathmandu

Translated from German

Two appointments were on the agenda today: first, the city sightseeing, and second, the meeting with Sunrise.

After sleeping in and a tasty breakfast we met our contacts from the agency. The boss discussed rebooking our tickets with us (as we wanted to fly earlier), and his colleague, a 66-year-old "freelancer", was to show us Kathmandu. Shiva, as he's called, speaks German and is very nice.

Over the following 4.5 hours we saw 3 sights. First a Buddhist temple, Swayambhunath. It's known as the Monkey Temple because monkeys run around everywhere. The stupa is one of the oldest Buddhist temple complexes in the world. Parts are estimated to be over 2,500 years old. Believers and pilgrims walk around everywhere, turning the prayer wheels, making offerings and praying. And the greedy monkeys then pinch the fruit and vegetable offerings back out of the shrines.

Then we visited the most important Hindu temple complex in Kathmandu, Pashupatinath. The Hindu temple lies on the sacred Bagmati river. There the dead are cremated on the bank and their ashes poured into the river. A cremation like this uses butter and logs and takes about 3–4 hours. Next to it, lots of small children play in the water. They jump into the dark brew or fish for coins with magnets. Contact with death seems very direct here, so it's not surprising that nobody really seems to take part. Nobody objects to the process being photographed or filmed, either. To our way of thinking that's very strange.

Incidentally, pregnant women, children under 5 and people who died of a snakebite are not cremated but buried.

Lots of so-called mendicant monks were also "hanging around" the temple complex, dressed in bright orange and painted chalk-white, with long hair and beards, lying around somewhere. They're not real monks but beggars. They put on a show. But we also saw a few people with leprosy. They were real – you can't fake that. Very upsetting.

The third temple complex we visited is Boudha. It's one of the largest Buddhist stupas. Pilgrims always walk around a stupa clockwise, keeping it on their right. Several times, if they wish. The structure itself is solid; there's no room inside.

After a short lunch our tour ended back at the hotel.

In the afternoon – at about 3:30 – Emma Taylor and her Sunrise co-founder picked us up. The drive in a small car took us to the house where the children live. After about 30 minutes we were there – just as the school bus arrived with the children.

Sunrise rents 2 houses for the children, one for the little boys and the girls and one for the older boys. The house for the little ones is a bit reminiscent of Pippi Longstocking's Villa Villekulla. Really very funny.

The little ones greeted us with a friendly "Namaste" (hello) and asked us our names straight out. They were all wearing school uniforms. Very cute. Then they quickly disappeared into Villa Villekulla while Emma told us about the overall situation. As we stood in front of the house, the little ones came pouring out again. They had changed and were now wearing their play clothes. They were all very cheerful and jumped around the garden. There's also a kind of shed there where they do their homework. All very, very simple and without light.

The house itself is a villa. In one room, for example, 11 boys sleep on 2 double bunk beds. Next to them is a bed where the house mother sleeps. Then there are further rooms for the girls and one for the volunteer. There's also a common room and a training room for physiotherapy – because some children have physical disabilities. All very, very basic. But the children are very cheerful. You can see that they're happy. I don't even want to imagine what they've already been through.

Emma and her colleague are doing a great job. Every child threw their arms around their necks several times. Everyone laughs a lot and has fun. The children also look after their own things and are well-behaved. Without being asked, they withdrew to the shed shortly afterwards to do their homework.

Cooking and eating happen in the neighbouring house, where the big boys live. This building is in even poorer condition. But here, too, the boys are very happy and make a good impression. By the way, avocados, pomelos, guavas and other exotic fruits grow in the garden.

All the children go to a school about 15 minutes away by bus. Sunrise pays the school fees. The children learn English there from first grade.

After about 1.5 hours we were taken back to the hotel. I've been asked to pass on best wishes and a heartfelt thank-you to all the other donors.

Ralf and I rounded off the day at the roulette table in the casino next door. I had no luck, unfortunately, but Ralf all the more. He's always lucky. :)

Tomorrow the plane goes back to Dubai. We're already looking forward to it.
