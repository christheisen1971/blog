DEENTR

Day 4 · September 21, 2009

Namche Bazaar – 3,450 m

Translated from German

The first real trekking day began with a more or less cold quick wash. The only washing place is in the lodge yard – outside, that is. Luckily it's still fairly bearable in September – I just wonder how trekkers manage in winter. Admittedly the washing place has a pretty impressive view of a mountain range. So you do end up brushing your teeth for a full 3 minutes. After washing, getting dressed and packing the duffel bag, it was time for breakfast. We're still walking together with the other groups, as the route to Namche Bazaar is the same for everyone. Only then do the paths divide.

As expected, today was a hard day with a net ascent of 800 metres to our destination, Namche Bazaar. Net, because – and I hadn't realised this until today – on some stretches you descend hundreds of metres only to climb again. That's very sobering, because the climb is already exhausting. And when you've climbed 100 metres and then lose 80 again after the next bend – which is just as tiring, by the way – it's annoying. But of course a route like this makes sense, since we're crossing gorges and there aren't suspension bridges everywhere.

Which brings me to the keyword: suspension bridges. Well, I didn't know there were bridges like these here either. Some are so high that even I get dizzy. Of course it's great fun to walk across these steel structures and look at the raging river beneath you. But when the wind picks up, the bridge sways a little and maybe a rider, a few cattle or Sherpas with loads come towards you, it does get a bit unnerving.

Since it was warm and sunny, I walked in a T-shirt and cap and long trekking trousers (I've only brought one pair). We also put on our boots today, and that was important.

The first half-stage again led uphill and downhill over stony paths past many guesthouses. Sometimes there are almost steps built in, but made of uneven pieces of rock and never the same height. That makes it hard to find a rhythm when climbing. Imagine the ascent as a long, steep staircase with big steps made of rock. But every step has to be considered. The paths are 1–4 metres wide, and at the side it drops away quite steeply, although trees or bushes often still grow there. The vegetation is very green, with streams, flowers and animals everywhere. Every few metres a Sherpa with a load or a hiker comes by.

By the way, the Sherpas get about 5 rupees per kilo for a stage like this. That's about 5 cents. One litre of water in a PET bottle costs 50–150 rupees, for comparison. The Sherpas – sadly often children too – carry incredible loads. Unimaginable. And they walk in flip-flops or trainers – surely also because proper trekking boots are beyond their means.

At Tawa we entered Sagarmatha National Park today – Mount Everest's actual name is Sagarmatha. The whole region is a nature reserve and protected accordingly. By the way, there are three 8,000-metre peaks in the Khumbu: Mount Everest (8,848 m), Lhotse (8,516 m) and Cho Oyu (8,201 m). To enter the national park you need a form with a photo. Along the way there are checkpoints you pass. If I understood correctly, this also lets the authorities find out whether hikers have gone missing – we certainly saw missing-person posters.

We had lunch in a nice guesthouse with a lovely flower garden. There's basically always the same choice: fried rice or noodles with vegetables and/or meat, spaghetti, sandwiches, pizza, meat dishes, salads etc. We had been advised against eating meat, as there's no slaughtering up there and everything has to be carried up for hours. So a proper cold chain isn't possible. And salads and raw fruit are taboo anyway. I mainly eat lots of rice or noodles here to get enough carbohydrates. You burn an incredible amount.

We drink a lot too. 4–5 litres are a must, because you sweat a lot. But you also need the fluid because it thins the blood the higher we go. It helps against dehydration and headaches.

Headaches can come from the wind and the changed air pressure. But they can also be a sign of beginning altitude sickness. And that can quickly turn fatal. There's medication for the pain, but really only one thing helps: descend immediately. Only that's not always possible, especially at night. And helicopters don't always reach the emergency quickly, or can't come at all because of the weather. Some in the group already have mild headaches; aspirin helps best – not least because of its blood-thinning effect. Luckily I don't have anything yet. Still, we notice that breathing is getting harder and we run out of breath more quickly.

After the lunch break came the hardest part. It was a very long climb, almost 3 hours in one go. Unfortunately our guide Ram (see photo, centre) had failed to tell us that – unlike before – no water would be available until our destination, so before long we had nothing left to drink. And that was fatal. Ram then had someone come down to meet us with water from the destination. After that things went better again.

After 7–8 hours – I don't know exactly – we reached our goal, Namche Bazaar (3,450 m). It's a somewhat larger place with internet cafés, an ATM and shops. It's also the only one in the region this big, so if you need money, change it here (tip!). This is where the actual "loop" begins. We'll pass through again on the way back and take the same route to our starting point, Lukla.

When we arrived, the town – which sits on a slope and probably stretches over an estimated 50–100 metres of altitude – was already partly in a cloud. It was shady and damp. During our climb on the second half-stage it had already turned cool and we had reached the cloud line.

Our lodge in Namche Bazaar is above the town and once again extremely basic: the rooms are plywood partitions, grubby toilets in the corridor, a cold and draughty shower across the yard. The dining room is nice, with a great view of Namche Bazaar, and the food and tea are quite tasty, but there's an alternative: Stefan, a hiker from another group, came over and told us about his guesthouse in the town below our lodge, where he and his wife Sandra were staying. So today Ralf and I moved to this other place, especially as tomorrow is an acclimatisation day anyway and we'll be here longer. Alexander from our group didn't want to switch; Harald would have liked to come but didn't want to leave Alexander alone. So our little group is now split, which certainly isn't great, but on the other hand we have to be very careful with our health. And when you get the chance to stay in a clean, draught-free room with a bed, your own shower and your own toilet, in our view you should definitely take it. Because the whole thing is very draining, and every virus and every bacterium loves a weakened body. And the extra cost of about €5 per person per night hardly matters, especially as in the basic lodge you pay another €3 for a shower. And €1 for each hour of battery charging. In the other lodge all of that is included. Unfortunately we probably won't find any better lodges after Namche Bazaar.

So the day was marked by exertion, but also by very beautiful moments. We're slowly falling into the rhythm of Nepal.
