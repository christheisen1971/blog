DEENTR

Day 5 · July 28, 2011 · 5,137 m

Summit push

Translated from German

At 1 a.m. the alarm tore us out of an already uncomfortable sleep. It was pitch dark and freezing cold at 4,200 m. While in the city there is always some background glow of light, up there on the mountain it was truly black as night.

Under these circumstances, peeling yourself out of a cramped three-man tent, putting on your hiking gear and not falling off the slightly raised, far too small tent pitch was quite an art. Of course none of us was really hungry. But there was no other way: we had to drink something hot and eat something nourishing. Shivering and dead tired, we huddled in the tiny kitchen tent, drank black tea and ate sheets of flatbread thickly spread with Nutella.

Then at 1:41 a.m. we set off. With backpacks full of camera gear, water, chocolate, nuts, crampons and ice axes on our backs and headlamps on our heads, we crept up the mountain in single file. The path was frozen. Apart from the 20 centimetres in front of you, you could see absolutely nothing.

First we went about 50 metres up through the camp and past the tents of the other groups. Then we crossed a field of larger boulders. And finally a narrow path led steeply uphill the whole way. At first our guide let the slowest of us, Fethiye, go in front. She did well, too. But she wasn't well equipped: she had neither a trekking pole nor a backpack that distributes the weight onto the hips. So after about an hour she had to give up. She stayed behind and went back down to Camp 2 with one of the guides.

Again and again we had to take a break. At this altitude the body gets very little oxygen. It's not that you breathe heavily; you are simply exhausted. Step by step we went uphill. Slowly we noticed that the sun would soon rise. Of course we wanted to be on the summit at sunrise, but it was clear that we wouldn't make it. Besides, we still hoped to reach the snow areas early enough not to need crampons. So we kept up a good pace. I had had Endomondo running on the iPhone the whole time. After 2:39 hours, at 4,738 m, the battery unfortunately ran low. So I switched the phone off. At roughly this altitude we also reached the snow. Luckily it was still frozen enough that we didn't need the crampons.

During the ascent we didn't have much chance to turn around and enjoy the view. But now that it was getting lighter and lighter, we did so every now and then. And so at 5:20 a.m. we saw, for the first time, the shadow of Ararat on the plateau. An unforgettable sight that a photo can only capture poorly.

The summit came closer and closer. A group that had already been at the top came towards us and wished us luck. The last stretch was a dream in white. And it was cold, very cold. So far we were lucky to be walking in a kind of lee. But that was about to change.

At 6:40 a.m. we reached the summit at 5,137 m. Compared to Nepal, for example Kala Patthar, Ararat is fairly plain. You can see very far, but there are simply no other mountains nearby. From Kala Patthar you could admire dozens of mountains and, above all, the neighbouring, majestic Mount Everest and its glacier. On Ararat you "only" see the slightly lower second summit and "Little Ararat". After all the hardship, though, we weren't thinking about such things. We enjoyed the white snow and the gently falling slopes, congratulated each other and the members of other groups ... and froze. Supposedly it is -10 degrees Celsius up there, but with the strong wind sweeping across the summit it felt like -30.

So we took photos in a great hurry, because without gloves our hands threatened to freeze off. By the way, at the highest point there was a kind of guest book, in which some of our group signed their names. After about 20 minutes we started the descent. We wanted to get all the way down to base camp, 2,000 m lower, that same day.

The descent at first, in the snow, was pure joy. With huge leaps we ran down the mountain. But we soon reached the scree line, and now nothing was frozen any more. Little meltwater streams made the ground even softer. So the descent was – for me at least – very strenuous. Some coped better, others even worse, so the group became more and more spread out. Harald was of course the fittest and went ahead. Ahmet and I stayed together further back.

At this point I have to thank Ahmet, because I had real problems on the descent. At first I didn't know exactly what the cause was. Later I realised that I was dehydrated and that my field of vision had become very narrow. Normally, when descending, you keep a longer stretch of 2–5 metres in view and plan the next 3–4 steps ahead. But when you are dehydrated, this shrinks to the next step, so you quickly start stumbling. I had no water left, only a small carton of a sweet drink. After that, though, I felt much better. I made it to Camp 2 without anyone's help, but knowing that Ahmet was always with me.

At Camp 2 the helpers – contrary to what we had been told – had already taken down and loaded all the tents. But there had still been personal things in the tents that, in the morning rush, we naturally hadn't packed away. The helpers had thrown everything into some bags or other. My mood, which wasn't good anyway because of the difficult descent, didn't exactly improve.

We took a break of about 30–40 minutes at the camp. Then we continued to base camp, where we wanted to spend the night. Refreshed and "hydrated", we walked down this stretch for the second time. And what had to happen, happened: Ahmet looked for his 2 great flat "hot" stones, which he had found the day before and hidden by the side of the path, in order to lug them all the way down the mountain. One stone surely weighed 5–7 kilos, the other 15–17 kilos. He put the small stone in his backpack and the big one in a sack he had borrowed from the cook. Mind you: that day we had already climbed 1,000 metres up and 1,000 metres down, and had another 1,000 metres ahead of us. After a few hundred metres I couldn't bear to watch any longer and took over the smaller of the two stones. If someone is crazy enough to do something like that, then it is my duty to help him.

Around midday we reached base camp, dead tired, and were welcomed by the 3 group members who hadn't made it. We spent the rest of the day resting, eating and packing. The next day we were to head down early again, because we still had some sightseeing ahead of us.
