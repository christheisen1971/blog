DEENTR

Day 6 · July 29, 2011

Return and sightseeing

Translated from German

Since we had hardly slept the night before, this night was short too. We wanted to get up early and descend, because we still had a lot planned for the day. So the alarm went off at 6 a.m. and breakfast followed shortly after. We packed our bits and pieces, and the tents were taken down and loaded, together with the kitchen tent and the luggage, onto the horses, which had arrived on time. By 6:45 a.m. we were already walking. The sun warmed us, and the lower we got, the more pleasant the walking became.

Hülya had problems after just a few metres. So Gökay had to look after her again and walked slowly downhill with her, while the rest of the group carried on as normal. Our goal was the handover point just before Eliköy, where we had left the bus and started walking a few days earlier. Along the way, at around 9 a.m., we passed the "Ararat Café" again and had something to drink.
Looking back, we have to say that we were very lucky with the weather. When we arrived, the summit had still been heavily covered in cloud. But from the moment we started heading for the summit, all the clouds had disappeared. On the summit itself the weather was perfect, if cold. And now, on our way back, the sun was shining too.

The whole descent took 4 hours, so we were at the bus stop shortly before 10 a.m. The bus was already waiting for us. The driver had brought a big watermelon, which he cut up and handed round. Very tasty. It took a while until all the group members had trickled in. We also had to wait for the horses with the luggage. When they arrived, the luggage was reloaded, and Ahmet had fun getting photographed on the horse. There is also a photo of him in a fighting pose – apparently he was bursting with adrenaline. The others then had photos taken on the horse too – the poor animal. As if it hadn't had enough to carry already.

The bus ride back to the hotel was bumpy again and took a while. On the way we picked up a pedestrian. You have to imagine that people there walk a lot and then – whenever a car or bus comes by – somehow get taken along. Our bus was already full to bursting, but the good man simply stood on the ladder at the back of the bus that led up to the roof on the outside.

At the hotel, Harald, Ahmet and I treated ourselves to the luxury of a double room each. Why we didn't think of that on the first day is still a mystery to me. Anyway, we had to pay about 20 euros more per person – but it was worth it to us. Now everyone could take a long shower, which we hadn't been able to do for days.

Then we met in the lobby at 3 p.m. and took the minibus to Doğubeyazıt to stroll around a bit. I wanted to buy a carpet, and Ahmet and Harald wanted to go to the barber. The first stop was a carpet shop whose owners, a married couple, spoke perfect German. I picked out a small carpet and bought it. Then on to the barber. First Harald was shaved in a way he had probably never experienced before. In any case he was visibly pleased. Ahmet got the full programme: his beard and all sorts of other facial hair, which I'd rather not mention here, were removed with a razor and wax. All in all it took about 45 minutes, and as far as I know Ahmet paid 10 TL, i.e. less than 5 euros, for both of them. Incredible.

A little later we picked up the rest of the group at the hotel with the bus and first drove towards the Iranian border. There was the long queue of lorries in the right-hand lane in front of customs that is usual at such borders. Among other things, I saw a double-decker car transporter loaded with Porsche Panameras. The Persians clearly know how to appreciate quality.

Shortly before the border, though, we turned off and drove to a crater in Gürbulak, which was created by a meteorite impact in September 1892. With a diameter of 35 metres and a depth of 60 metres, it is the second-largest crater of its kind in the world.

Then we went on to the supposed site of Noah's Ark. This site is not directly on Ararat but about 20 km away in a hilly area. We couldn't go to the site itself, only to a viewpoint located a little higher up opposite it. From there you have a very good view, and the formation is certainly interesting.

But the surrounding area is really beautiful in general. I had imagined Eastern Anatolia to be much more barren and rugged. But it is a very beautiful, green, hilly region, shaped by Ararat and the foothills of the high Iranian mountains.

Our drive from Noah's Ark to the Ishak Pasha Palace took us along country roads through this beautiful, gently rolling hill country. The setting sun bathed everything in clear, warm colours. Everywhere you saw livestock and grain or grass already rolled into bales. If you assume that the volcano Ararat has made this soil very fertile and that there is probably a lot of groundwater in the plateau, it is a very rich region. Oil deposits are also suspected here. So it is understandable that Turkey doesn't simply want to give up this region.

Shortly before sunset we arrived at the Ishak Pasha Palace, which however was already closed. So we sat in a café a little higher up, drank tea and enjoyed the sunset and the view of the palace. They have evidently tried not simply to rebuild the missing elements of the palace true to the original, but have chosen a solution using foreign materials such as steel and glass, so that old and new can be told apart more easily. A thoroughly successful approach. The İshak Paşa Sarayı, as it is called in Turkish, is only about 300 to 400 years old, but it stands on the remains of a fortress that is about 2,800 years old. The present building combines various architectural styles of the region. The former golden gates are now in the Hermitage in St Petersburg. By the way, Ishak is an Islamic spelling of Isaac, just as Süleyman stands for Solomon or Davut for David.

After the stop at the palace we returned to the hotel. Dinner was already waiting for us. We were all hungry. Ahmet, Harald and I had earlier told our guide Gökay about the "Radler" or "Alsterwasser" (beer mixed with lemonade) and wanted to make him one. However, instead of Fanta or Sprite, the hotel only had a rather sweet lemonade, with which our Turkish Radler didn't taste good at all. Accordingly, Gökay wasn't very convinced. He will probably never try it again in his life.

We all ended the evening together on the roof terrace with a last tea. We had now got up very early two days in a row, and the next day we had to be up very early too, because the first participants – Harald among them – were due to fly out in the late morning from Van, still a good 2 hours away. So we went to our rooms fairly soon.
