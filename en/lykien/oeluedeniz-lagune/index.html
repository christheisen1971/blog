A day at the Ölüdeniz lagoon – and a twisted ankle while jogging

Translated from German

The Green Pine Hotel sits right on the bay of Ölüdeniz. The lagoon looks like a lake: the water lies calm between pine forest and steep mountains. In the distance we could see the motorised paragliders above the bay.

On the morning of 25 September Lütfü and I went for a run: 2.59 kilometres along the beach at Ölüdeniz, in about 20 minutes at 21 degrees. Unfortunately I twisted my ankle.

The bay of Ölüdeniz

The bay of Ölüdeniz near Fethiye is one of the best-known landscapes on Turkey's Mediterranean coast. It is not only beautiful but also interesting in terms of geology, language and history.

A "Dead Sea" full of life

Ölüdeniz literally means "Dead Sea". That sounds odd, because the bay is full of life. The name refers to the unusually calm water: while waves roll on the open sea outside, the lagoon often stays almost as smooth as a mirror. Today the name stands both for the coastal town and for the famous lagoon, often called the "Blue Lagoon".

How the lagoon was formed

A long, narrow spit of sand and gravel largely separates the lagoon from the open Mediterranean. It is not a freshwater lake, though: a narrow opening connects it to the sea. The pale seabed and the clear water create its typical turquoise and blue tones.

The legend of Belcekız

A sad love story surrounds the name. A ship was caught in a storm off the coast. The captain's son knew of a sheltered bay and wanted to steer the ship there. But his father believed the boy only wanted to visit his sweetheart ashore. In the quarrel he struck him with an oar; the young man fell overboard and drowned. When the storm died down, the captain saw that his son had been right: behind the spit lay a perfectly calm bay. His sweetheart, Belcekız, is said to have thrown herself from the cliffs in despair. That is how the legend explains the names Ölüdeniz and Belcekız – the story itself is not documented.

Ölüdeniz and the Lycians

Ölüdeniz lies in the west of ancient Lycia, not far from Telmessos, today's Fethiye. Its sheltered position probably appealed to sailors even in antiquity. There is, however, no evidence that the lagoon itself was an important Lycian harbour or a larger settlement. The surrounding area has many ancient remains, and the Lycian Way runs past the bay high above the coast.

Right behind Ölüdeniz rises Babadağ, almost 2,000 metres high – today an internationally known launch site for paragliders. The lagoon and parts of the coast are protected; pine forests, maquis and steep limestone mountains are typical.
