Boat trip to Gemiler Adası, the island of Saint Nicholas

Translated from German

In the morning we had a visitor: a cat had made itself comfortable on our veranda. Then we had breakfast.

After breakfast we headed out to sea on a yacht, with drinks on board. In between we jumped into the water for a swim. Along the way we passed some of the party boats that cruise from bay to bay here with music and lots of guests.

Then we headed for Gemiler Adası.

Lütfü and I went ashore and wandered through the ruins.

Gemiler Adası – the island of Saint Nicholas

Gemiler Adası is only about a kilometre long. It lies some nine kilometres south of Fethiye, less than 100 metres off the coast, and is uninhabited today. There are different explanations for the name: "island of boats" – fitting for Saint Nicholas, patron saint of sailors – or from the Greek word for "camel", because of the island's shape. Medieval Italian sea charts call it St Nicholas Island.

A Byzantine pilgrim stop

The ruins date from Byzantine times and are around 1,500 years old. Between the 4th and 6th centuries five churches were built here, along with around 40 other buildings and more than 50 Christian graves. The island flourished in the 6th century, sustained by Mediterranean sea trade and the veneration of Saint Nicholas. It lay on the route of Christian pilgrims to the Holy Land: ships called here to take on water and supplies, and the pilgrims prayed in the churches before sailing on.

Who lived here?

On the north side lay a small town of houses, cisterns and churches – without the theatres, baths or market squares familiar from ancient cities. A Christian community lived here, making its living from shipping and pilgrims. Remains of mosaics and frescoes survive, along with a large arched cistern and a processional way about 350 metres long leading up to the main church and the island's highest point. There, at the western end of the way, is a church carved into the rock.

Saint Nicholas's first tomb?

Archaeologists think it possible that Saint Nicholas was first buried in the rock church after his death in the 4th century. His remains are said to have lain here until the 650s. Then an Arab fleet threatened the coast, the inhabitants left the island, and the relics were taken to Myra, today's Demre. The island has been deserted ever since. It has been studied mainly by Japanese archaeologists, who have worked here since the 1990s and, among other things, uncovered floor mosaics of high quality.

Today the island belongs to the visitors who arrive by boat – and to the goats.

Back on board we raised our cups one more time.
