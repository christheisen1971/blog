Stage 1 · Hiking day 3 · 29 September 2025

Rain above the Butterfly Valley – and a 30th wedding anniversary

Translated from German

On the third day we started in Faralya, under an overcast sky and on a wet road.

In the gardens of Faralya hung pomegranates and still-green citrus fruit, probably oranges that only ripen in winter. By the path grew a large prickly pear cactus (Opuntia), behind which the land dropped steeply to the sea. It originally comes from the Americas but is now at home all around the Mediterranean; its fruit, the prickly pears (frenk inciri in Turkish), are edible but covered in fine spines.

Soon the view opened up over the Butterfly Valley (Kelebekler Vadisi): a narrow bay with a sandy beach between steep, pale rock walls.

We continued towards Kabak.

The path ran high above the cliffs, with nothing but open sea below us.

By the path grew shrubs with spiny leaves reminiscent of holly. This is the kermes oak (Quercus coccifera), one of the most typical shrubs of the maquis – with the same fat acorns we had already seen on the first hiking day.

After midday we continued towards Kabak. Yeşim took a short break on a rock, the cliffs and the sea behind her.

In the middle of the path we came across a tortoise. The species living here is mainly the spur-thighed tortoise (Testudo graeca), tosbağa in Turkish. It is protected and lives to a remarkable age, often several decades. Ours immediately withdrew into its shell.

Lycian tombs in the pine forest

Shortly afterwards we stood among the pines in front of ancient sarcophagi right by the path – one of them even has the red-and-white Lycian Way marker at its foot. They are typical Lycian sarcophagi: a stone chest on a base, topped by a heavy lid shaped like a pointed arch. The shape recalls the wooden houses of the Lycians and upturned ship hulls. Tomb robbers broke the chests open long ago.

On one of the sarcophagi an inscription in Greek letters survives, framed by a panel with spiral ornaments. Funerary inscriptions like this from Hellenistic and Roman times usually name the builder and the people allowed to be buried in the tomb – and often a fine for anyone who puts someone in without permission. Sadly we could not read the weathered text.

The path led back to the sea, and below us lay a bay with turquoise water and a few boats. By the path the sea squill was flowering once more, as we had seen it on the first hiking day.

It had already rained once along the way, and we had to put on our rain gear.

At the end, in Kabak, the rain caught us properly once more. Nesli took it with humour.

At the end the watch showed 10.88 kilometres and 373 metres of ascent.

Measured with an Apple Watch

The tour's WhatsApp group also provided a small piece of lore for this day: the carob tree (keçiboynuzu). Its seeds are said to weigh almost exactly the same, around 0.2 grams, which is why they were once used as a unit of weight. The word “carat” goes back to them as well: it comes from the Greek keration, the carob seed.

In the evening we celebrated our 30th wedding anniversary with our travel group.

Then Deniz stood up and gave a very loving speech about her friendship with Yeşim. Both of them even ended up in tears – and finally in each other's arms.

Afterwards I gave a little speech of my own:

Unfortunately my Turkish is very limited. Okay.

I first came here 35 years ago. That was in 1990. I was 19 or 20 at the time. I met Yeşim and fell in love with her. She was studying here in Turkey back then, and I was studying in Germany. For three or four years we kept visiting each other. And at some point I said to her: "Come to Germany!" And she came to me. I stole her from Turkey, so to speak.

But in return I have gained an incredible number of new friends from Turkey. I used to be a real German. Today I'm at least half German and half Turkish.

You have a beautiful, wonderful country. And you, the people of Turkey, are simply great. I am always welcomed so warmly here – by my Turkish family, by all my Turkish friends. Starting with Deniz, my first friend here. And I am so happy that I can be part of you.

Thank you so, so much! Cheers! Şerefe!
