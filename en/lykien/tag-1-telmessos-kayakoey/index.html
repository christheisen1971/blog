DEENTR

Stage 1 · Hiking day 1 · 27 September 2025

Rock tombs, an abandoned village and the way to Ölüdeniz

Translated from German

The guided tour began on 27 September. Part of the group had only landed at Dalaman that morning and was picked up there; together we set off for the first stage. From this day on we stayed at the Kaizen Gate Hotel in Ölüdeniz.

The first stop of the tour was Telmessos, today's Fethiye. Above the town the Lycian rock tombs are cut into the cliff, and in front of the largest of them, the Tomb of Amyntas, the whole group lined up for a photo.

Of course we also took a few souvenir photos.

Telmessos – the city beneath today's Fethiye

Walking through Fethiye today, you are walking over one of the oldest cities in Lycia. Telmessos, Telebehi in Lycian, lay on the Gulf of Fethiye, on Lycia's western border with Caria. Its harbour was well sheltered by an island in front of it and was considered the most important in the west of the country. The westernmost known inscription in the Lycian language was found here too.

From the Hittites to the Romans

Hittite texts from the 13th century BC already mention a place called Kuwalapašša in the land of Lukka, probably Telmessos. The first settlement lay on the Hıdırlık hills; only later did the town move down to the shore. Around 547 BC the region came under Persian rule. In the 5th century BC Telmessos belonged to the Delian League and paid more into it than any other Lycian city. At times Lycian dynasts such as Arbinas ruled the city – the same Arbinas who appears on the Nereid Monument frieze on this blog's home page.

In 334 BC Alexander the Great took Telmessos. After his death it changed hands several times: the Ptolemies, the Seleucids and the kingdom of Pergamon. In the Hellenistic period Telmessos was one of the largest and richest port cities in the region. Around 80 BC the city joined the Lycian League, and in AD 43 it became part of the Roman province of Lycia et Pamphylia.

In antiquity Telmessos was famous for its seers. Aristander of Telmessos accompanied Alexander the Great on his campaigns as his soothsayer. There was, however, also a Telmessos in Caria, and in some ancient reports it is not entirely clear which of the two is meant.

The Tomb of Amyntas

The Tomb of Amyntas is the largest and most important rock tomb in Telmessos. It was made in the second half of the 4th century BC, around 350 BC. The façade measures about 11 by 13 metres and imitates an Ionic temple: two columns between two pilasters, with a triangular pediment above. Behind it lies a burial chamber that is unusually large for a rock tomb.

A Greek inscription names the dead man: "Amyntou tou Hermagiou" – "(the tomb) of Amyntas, son of Hermagios". Amyntas is a Greek name, while his father's name is Lycian. Little is known about Amyntas himself; he may have been a local ruler or a wealthy member of the elite. The temple façade shows how strongly Greek models shaped Lycian art at the time. Most of the other tombs on the slope follow the typical Lycian house style instead, imitating wooden houses in stone; some bear inscriptions in Lycian.

Not everything carved into the stone is ancient: later visitors immortalised themselves on the façade with names and dates.

What else remains of Telmessos

In the middle of town stands a Lycian sarcophagus with a pointed-arch lid decorated with battle scenes. In the 19th century it still stood in the harbour with its base in the water; today it stands next to the government building. The ancient theatre lies by the harbour. Most of its stones were used to build the harbour in 1953; it has been under restoration since 2012.

From Telmessos to Fethiye

In Byzantine times Telmessos was a bishop's seat. In the 8th century the city was briefly called Anastasioupolis, later Makri, after the island in front of the harbour. Under the Turks this became Meğri. The Turks arrived in the late 12th or early 13th century, the Menteşe principality in 1284 and the Ottomans in 1424. In 1934 the city got its present name, Fethiye, in honour of the early Ottoman aviator Fethi Bey. In the population exchange after the First World War the Greek inhabitants left the town. The 1957 earthquake destroyed most of its buildings – one reason why so little of the ancient city can be seen apart from the tombs.

Kayaköy – the abandoned village

At around half past one we reached Kayaköy, the second stop of the day. This was also where our actual hike began, right through the ruins.

Kayaköy lies in a valley a few kilometres south of Fethiye. On the hillside the walls of around 500 houses stand close together, roofless, many still with chimneys and window openings. Today the village is protected as a historical monument.

Levissi: a Greek town on the hillside

The name goes back to the ancient settlement of Karmylessos, shortened to Lebessos. This became the Greek name Levissi (also Livissi); Kaya or Kayaköy is the Turkish name. Much of what you see today was built in the 18th century. After the earthquake of 1856 and a great fire in Fethiye in 1885, the town was renewed and kept growing.

At the beginning of the 20th century several thousand people lived here, Greek Orthodox Christians. The town had more than 20 churches and chapels, among them the "upper" church of Taxiarches and the "lower" church of Panagia Pyrgiotissa.

The end of Levissi

From 1914, during the First World War, the Greek inhabitants were persecuted and deported; many men died in the Ottoman labour battalions. After the Greco-Turkish War the last Greek residents left in 1922 and 1923. In 1923 the Treaty of Lausanne set out the population exchange: Orthodox Christians from Turkey had to go to Greece, Muslims from Greece to Turkey. The newcomers, however, hardly settled in Levissi, and the village fell into ruin. The 1957 earthquake damaged many of the empty houses further.

Kayaköy today

Today it is mostly hikers and day visitors who come. Only a few houses have been restored and are lived in; down in the valley lies the new Kayaköy with just under a thousand inhabitants. The British author Louis de Bernières is said to have been inspired by Levissi for his novel "Birds Without Wings".

On one wall hung the picture of a young woman – probably a reminder of the people who once lived here.

From Kayaköy to Ölüdeniz

From Kayaköy the path climbed through pine forest and maquis. Soon the view opened up over the sea: bays, headlands and small islands below us, with excursion boats drawing long wakes in between.

Yeşim was clearly in a good mood.

In places the path ran over bare limestone, furrowed by rain and weather.

In the late afternoon the Ölüdeniz lagoon lay below us – the water we had been pedalling across two days earlier.

That is also where we took a photo of the five of us.

What grows by the path

Along the way I mainly photographed plants and fruit. At the end of September the maquis is dry, and yet many shrubs are bearing fruit right now.

Olive tree. Olive trees keep appearing among the pines, often feral or wild forms with small fruit (delice in Turkish). The olives in my photo were already dark and wrinkled. The olive has been cultivated in the eastern Mediterranean for thousands of years and belongs to this landscape like hardly any other plant.

Evergreen oak. The shrub with small, grey-green, slightly wavy leaves and fat acorns is an evergreen oak – probably the kermes oak (Quercus coccifera) or a close relative. The kermes oak is one of the typical shrubs of the maquis. It is named after the kermes scale insect that lives on it, from which a bright red dye used to be made – hence the word "crimson".

Sea squill. The tall candle with its many white star-shaped flowers is a sea squill (Drimia maritima, ada soğanı in Turkish). Its bulb can grow as big as a head and often sits half above ground. In late summer it sends up a flower stalk of up to more than a metre, before the leaves appear; the flowers open from the bottom upwards. The plant is poisonous and was used in antiquity both as a medicine and as rat poison.

Mastic tree. The shrub with pinnate leaves and red berries is a mastic tree (Pistacia lentiscus, sakız ağacı in Turkish), a relative of the pistachio. The fruit is red at first and later turns black. Its resin gives us mastic, famous above all from the Greek island of Chios; in Turkey it flavours desserts, puddings and ice cream.

Silver-felted plant. This plant with whitish-grey, felty leaves was growing right on the rock. Which species it is cannot be said for sure from the photo. The dense felt of hairs, however, is typical of plants in sunny, dry places: it reflects light and protects against evaporation.

Spiny molucca balm. The dry stems with tiers of spiny calyces probably belong to the spiny molucca balm (Moluccella spinosa), a member of the mint family. After flowering the thorny calyces remain and dry in the sun into little stars like these.
